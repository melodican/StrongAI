By the winter of 1944, the German soldier on the Western Front had seen almost everything war
could throw at him. He had faced Soviet tank waves rolling across the steppe in the east. He
had endured the drone of Allied bombers passing overhead toward the cities of the Reich. He
had learned, the way all soldiers eventually learn, to live alongside fear and keep functioning
anyway. But ask the men who survived what they dreaded most — not what killed the most of them,
but what reached into their chests and squeezed the courage out of them — and the answer was
almost always the same. It wasn't the tanks. It wasn't the planes. It was the sky itself
tearing open without warning. It was American artillery. This is the dossier on the weapon that
broke the German army's nerve. This… is The War Dossier.

To understand that fear, you first have to understand what made American gunnery different,
because on the surface, artillery was artillery. Every major army in the war used it. The
Germans were masters of it, with superb guns and superbly trained crews. The Soviets fired it
in quantities that beggared belief, massing thousands of barrels for a single offensive. So
what could the Americans possibly do with their guns that would single them out, in the memory
of their enemies, as uniquely terrifying? The answer was not a bigger shell or a longer-ranged
gun. It was something far harder to build and far harder to copy. The Americans could take
dozens of batteries, scattered across miles of countryside, all pointing in different
directions, and make every single shell from every single gun land on the same patch of earth
at the same instant.

They called it "Time on Target." The principle was deceptively simple to state, and brutally
hard to achieve. A shell fired from a gun far away takes longer to travel to the target than a
shell fired from a gun close by. So the American fire-direction centers calculated, for every
gun in range, exactly how long its shell would spend in the air. Then they ordered each gun to
fire at a different moment — the most distant guns first, the nearest guns last — timed so
precisely that all the shells, regardless of where they had come from, arrived together, in a
single, annihilating crash.

For the men on the receiving end, this erased the one thing that had kept them alive. With
ordinary artillery, you learned to listen. The first ranging rounds would come in, searching
for the target, and that gave you a second, maybe two, to throw yourself into a hole. Soldiers
survived for years on that instinct, on that sliver of warning carved out of the noise. A Time
on Target strike took it away. There were no ranging shots. There was no walking of the fire
toward you. One moment the field was silent. The next, the entire world detonated at once,
everywhere, with no overture and no mercy.

German prisoners described it again and again to their interrogators, and the descriptions have
a striking sameness to them. Veterans who had stood firm under every other kind of fire
admitted, often with shame, that they could not hold a position under it. Men who had never run
in their lives, ran. The strikes did not merely kill the men they hit. They unmade something in
the men who survived — the basic human ability to anticipate danger, to prepare for it, to feel
even the smallest measure of control over one's own fate. And a soldier who has lost that is a
soldier who is halfway to broken.

[MID-ROLL]

What made all of this possible was not a wonder weapon you could photograph. It was a system,
which is exactly why the Germans found it so hard to answer. The Americans had built an
artillery network knitted together by radio and field telephone, run by trained forward
observers who could call down the fire of an entire division onto a single point on a map
within minutes. Picture one of those observers: a single man, crouched in the attic of a
shattered farmhouse, with a radio and a pair of binoculars. He spots a German company forming
up for a counterattack. He reads off a map reference. And minutes later, the combined fury of a
hundred guns falls on that spot, summoned by one voice. The Germans had brilliant gunners and
excellent weapons. What they lacked was that web — the instant, flexible coordination that let
a lone observer wield the firepower of a hundred barrels.

And behind the system stood the factories. American industry produced shells in quantities the
Germans found almost impossible to comprehend. A single US division, on a heavy day of fighting,
could fire tens of thousands of rounds. German batteries, increasingly starved of ammunition as
the war ground on, had to ration every shell, choosing their targets with painful care. The
Americans had no such worry. If a German position so much as revealed itself — a muzzle flash, a
moving vehicle, a glint of light — it could be smothered under a deluge of steel. The German
gunner counted his rounds. The American gunner counted his targets.

There is a grim and revealing detail from the fighting in Normandy. German officers, walking
their positions after an American barrage to recover their dead, sometimes found bodies with no
wounds at all. The men had not been torn apart. They had been killed by the concussion alone —
the overpressure of dozens of shells bursting together in an instant, rupturing lungs and
stopping hearts without leaving a single mark on the skin. To a soldier, the idea of a death
that leaves no wound carries its own special horror. It meant the fire did not even need to
touch you to kill you.

But the deepest effect of American artillery was not in the men it killed. It was in how it
reshaped the way the entire German army fought in the west. German commanders learned, through
hard experience, to move only at night, to dig deeper than they ever had before, to avoid
massing their men in the open where a single observer could doom them all. The threat of that
fire made them cautious. It made them slow. It robbed them of the bold, fast, concentrated
counterattacks that had won them so many victories in the war's early years — because to mass
for such an attack was to invite annihilation before the attack could even begin. A weapon that
changes how your enemy thinks, that shapes his every decision, is worth more than one that
simply kills. American artillery did both.

By the time of the Battle of the Bulge, in the winter of 1944, the lesson had been carved deep
into the German soldier's mind. During the siege of Bastogne, when German forces nearly
encircled the American defenders, it was massed American artillery — fired with that same
merciless, coordinated precision — that broke German attack after German attack before it could
close with the lines. German assault units that stepped off into the snow-covered open found
themselves swallowed by fire that seemed to know exactly where they were before they had even
begun to move. They were not fighting men they could see. They were fighting a math problem
being solved by unseen hands miles away, and the answer to that problem was their own
destruction.

So why did the German soldier fear American artillery more than any other weapon? Not because it
was the single deadliest thing on the battlefield — tanks killed, aircraft killed, machine guns
killed. He feared it because it took away the one thing a soldier needs to endure combat at all:
the belief that his own skill and courage could keep him alive. Against the bombers, you could
hide. Against the tanks, you could fight back, or run, or hunt them in turn. But against a
strike that arrived from everywhere at once, without warning, summoned by a voice on a radio you
would never see, there was nothing to do but press your face into the earth and pray, and wait,
and hope the math had not chosen you.

The German veterans who lived through it carried that sound with them for the rest of their
lives — the single, world-ending crash of a hundred guns speaking as one. It was the sound of an
enemy who had turned artillery from a weapon into a system, and in doing so had found a way to

The German tankers had a nickname for the American Sherman. They called it the "Tommy cooker,"
and later, with grim battlefield humor, the "Ronson" — after a cigarette lighter whose slogan
promised it would light every time. The joke, of course, was about how readily the Sherman
seemed to catch fire when it was hit. To the crews of the mighty Tiger and the sleek, deadly
Panther, the American tank looked like a tall, thin, under-gunned box on tracks. Easy prey. And
yet, by the end of the war, that contempt had curdled into something much closer to dread. This
is the dossier on the tank the Germans laughed at — and what they discovered when they finally
climbed inside one. This… is The War Dossier.

On paper, the German contempt had real foundations. The Sherman's armor was thinner than a
Panther's. Its main gun, for much of the war, simply could not punch through the frontal armor
of a Tiger at normal combat range. In a straight duel — one tank against one tank, across an
open field, at long distance — the German machine usually won, and won decisively. German tank
aces racked up kill counts that became the stuff of legend, dozens of enemy tanks credited to a
single commander. By the narrow measure of the one-on-one duel, the Sherman was outclassed, and
every German tanker knew it.

But war is not fought as a series of fair duels across open fields. And when German crews
actually captured intact Shermans — and they captured a great many — and climbed inside to study
them, they began to understand something the kill counts had completely hidden. The Sherman had
not been designed to win duels. It had been designed to win a war. And those, it turns out, are
two very different things.

The first thing a German tanker noticed inside a captured Sherman was how it was made. German
tanks were marvels of engineering — and, too often, nightmares of complexity. The Panther's
elegant overlapping road wheels had to be removed in a precise sequence just to replace one that
sat in the middle of the row. Its transmission was brilliant in concept and notoriously fragile
in practice. When a German tank broke down — and they broke down often, especially the heavier
models pushed beyond their mechanical limits — it could take days, specialized parts, and a
proper workshop to fix, if it could be fixed in the field at all.

The Sherman was the opposite philosophy made steel. It was built to be repaired by ordinary men
with ordinary tools, often in the open, often under pressure. Its parts were standardized and
interchangeable. Its engine could be pulled and swapped. A Sherman knocked out in the morning
might be patched, repaired, and back in the fighting line within a day or two. German crews
examining the design recognized, with a professional's eye, that they were looking at a machine
built not for a museum or a parade ground, but for the brutal, grinding arithmetic of attrition
— a machine designed to be broken and fixed and broken and fixed again, indefinitely.

[MID-ROLL]

Then there was the inside. The Sherman was roomy, even comfortable, by cramped German standards.
The crew had decent visibility through good optics and well-placed periscopes. Later models had
a powered turret traverse that spun the gun onto a target faster than the Germans expected —
which mattered enormously in the kind of fighting that actually dominated the Western Front: not
long-range duels across open steppe, but close, sudden, chaotic clashes among the hedgerows of
Normandy and the narrow streets of shattered towns. In that close fighting, where the German
gun's long-range advantage simply evaporated, the Sherman could often bring its gun to bear
first. More than one confident German tanker died learning that the "easy prey" could turn and
fire faster than he could.

But the discovery that truly unsettled the German crews was not any single feature. It was the
implication of the whole machine taken together. The Sherman was clearly, deliberately designed
to be built in enormous numbers and shipped across an entire ocean. It was light enough to cross
most European bridges without collapsing them. It fit neatly onto standard railway cars and
landing craft. Every choice in its design whispered the same quiet, relentless message: there
are thousands more of me where I came from.

And there were. American factories built nearly fifty thousand Shermans. Germany, for all its
engineering genius, built only around thirteen hundred Tigers. Sit with that comparison for a
moment, because it is the entire war in two numbers. A German crew might destroy three, four,
five Shermans in a single engagement — a heroic, even legendary feat — and then watch, with a
slow and dawning horror, as still more Shermans appeared over the next ridge, and the ridge
after that. The Tiger that had won the duel would eventually throw a track, or run dry of fuel,
or be flanked and killed, and when it was gone, it was gone forever, because there was no
thirteen-hundred-and-first to replace it. The Sherman that lost the duel was simply one wave in
an ocean that never stopped coming.

Captured German documents and postwar interviews record the shift in attitude with real clarity.
Early in the war, German tankers spoke of the Sherman with open contempt. By the end, that
contempt had hardened into a grudging, fearful respect — not for the individual tank, which they
still considered inferior, but for what it represented. They came to understand a terrible truth:
they were not fighting a tank at all. They were fighting a production line. Every Sherman they
killed was replaced almost before its hull had cooled. Every Tiger they lost subtracted, forever,
from a total that could never be rebuilt.

And here is the detail that completes the picture. The Americans knew their tank's weaknesses
perfectly well — and rather than pretend otherwise, they adapted. They up-gunned some Shermans
with a high-velocity cannon that could finally threaten the big German cats. More importantly,
they refined their tactics. They learned never to send tanks forward alone. They fought in
platoons and companies, using numbers and movement: one tank fixing the enemy in place from the
front while others maneuvered around the flank to strike the thinner side armor, where even a
Tiger was vulnerable. German crews, studying the wrecks and the tactics that had produced them,
recognized exactly what they were seeing — the methods of an army that had calmly accepted its
tank could not win alone, and had built its entire way of fighting around that fact.

So what did German tankers discover inside captured American Shermans? They discovered they had
been laughing at the wrong thing entirely. They had judged the Sherman as a weapon, and as a
weapon it was unremarkable, even poor. But as one part of a vast machine — a machine of
factories, railroads, cargo ships, spare parts, mobile workshops, and replaceable crews — it
was very nearly unbeatable. The Tiger was the better tank. The Sherman was the better strategy.
And in the end, as it almost always does, the better strategy buried the better tank. If you

There is a moment described by more than one German fighter pilot in the final years of the war.
He climbs to altitude over the Reich. He levels out, scanning the sky. And there, ahead of him,
stretching from one horizon to the other, he sees the American bomber stream — hundreds,
sometimes more than a thousand four-engined bombers, packed into tight defensive boxes, their
aluminum skins glinting in the high, cold sun. And instead of rolling in to attack, he turns
away. Not out of cowardice. Out of arithmetic. This is the dossier on the bomber that broke the
will of the German air force. This… is The War Dossier.

To understand why an experienced, decorated German pilot would refuse that fight, you have to
understand what he was actually looking at. The American B-17 was called the Flying Fortress, and
it earned the name. A single bomber bristled with as many as thirteen heavy machine guns,
arranged to cover nearly every angle of approach — nose, tail, top, belly, and both sides. One
Fortress, alone, was a dangerous thing to attack. But the Americans never sent one alone, and
that was the entire point.

They flew in what they called the combat box — a stacked, staggered, three-dimensional formation
in which the defensive guns of every bomber overlapped with the guns of its neighbors. A fighter
diving into that formation was not attacking one bomber. It was flying into a cone of
concentrated fire thrown up by dozens of aircraft at once — a moving wall of steel hanging in
the sky. To press an attack home, a German pilot had to fly straight into that wall, hold his
aim steady long enough to actually hit a single bomber, and then break away and escape — all
while every gunner within range poured fire at him. The geometry was merciless, and the German
pilots understood geometry.

Early in the campaign, though, the Germans found an answer, and for a time it worked
frighteningly well. They learned to attack from dead ahead, head-on, where the bombers' forward
defenses were weakest and the closing speed was so high the gunners had almost no time to react.
They took a fearful toll. There were American raids that lost so many bombers in a single day
that the entire daylight bombing campaign hung in the balance, its future genuinely in doubt.
For a window of time, the German fighter force believed it could win — that it could make the
cost of daylight bombing so high that the Americans would be forced to stop.

And then came the thing that broke them. It was not a new bomber. It was a new fighter — and,
more importantly, a new range.

[MID-ROLL]

The American P-51 Mustang could do something that the conventional wisdom said a single-engine
fighter simply could not do. It could escort the bombers all the way to the target, deep inside
Germany, and all the way home again. Before the Mustang, the short-ranged Allied escort fighters
had to turn back partway, leaving the bombers to fly the most dangerous leg of the journey —
over Germany itself — entirely naked. The German fighters lived for that window. They timed their
attacks for the moment the escorts peeled away. The Mustang slammed that window shut. Suddenly,
everywhere the bombers went, the escorts went too, all the way there and all the way back.

Now the arithmetic turned completely against the German pilot. To reach the bombers, he first
had to fight through swarms of Mustangs flown by increasingly well-trained American pilots in a
superb aircraft. And the Americans, with cold deliberation, changed their tactics from defense to
slaughter. They released the Mustangs from close escort and sent them ranging ahead, above, and
below — not merely to protect the bombers, but to hunt. To catch German fighters as they
struggled to climb to altitude, to jump them as they formed up, to chase them down and kill them
as they came in to land, low and slow and out of fuel.

The result was an extermination of experience. Germany's veteran pilots — the irreplaceable core
of men who had flown since the early victories, who carried the skill and the cunning that only
combat survival can teach — began to die faster than they could possibly be replaced. And here
the German training system failed at the worst possible moment. As losses mounted and fuel grew
scarce, new German pilots were rushed to the front with fewer and fewer hours of flight training
— sometimes only a small fraction of what their American counterparts received before ever seeing
combat. A green German pilot, barely able to control his aircraft in a dogfight, was thrown
against a confident American who had trained for hundreds of hours. The outcome was rarely in
doubt, and the new pilots often did not survive their first few missions.

So by 1944, the veteran German pilot faced an equation with no good answer. If he attacked the
bombers, he had to fly through a wall of defensive fire and a screen of hunting Mustangs to do
it. If, by skill and luck, he survived, he would have to do it again tomorrow, and the day after,
and the day after that, until the odds finally found him. And his death would be replaced by a
teenager with twenty hours in the cockpit who would die on his first sortie. Some pilots, sitting
at altitude and looking at that bomber stream filling the sky from edge to edge, made the cold,
clear calculation that there was simply no point. They could not stop it. They could only feed
themselves into it, one by one, to no effect.

German records and postwar testimony capture the despair without flinching. Commanders wrote of
"the death of the fighter arm." Pilots described the bomber formations not as targets but as a
kind of weather — a force of nature to be endured rather than fought, like a storm you could not
turn back and could only hope to survive. The very confidence that had defined the Luftwaffe in
its years of glory drained away, replaced by a grim, settled certainty that the sky no longer
belonged to them, and never would again.

And that was the true and often-overlooked victory of the American bombing campaign. People count
the bombs that fell on factories and oil refineries, and those mattered. But the deeper
achievement was this: the campaign forced the German air force to come up and fight, on American
terms, in the air, where the Mustangs were waiting. And there it destroyed the one thing Germany
could never rebuild — its experienced pilots, and its will to contest the sky at all.

So why did German pilots refuse to engage American bomber formations? Because they had run the
numbers in their own heads, in the cockpit, at altitude, with the whole stream laid out in front
of them — and the numbers said the same thing every time. This was not a battle. It was an
execution, and they had been assigned the role of the condemned. If the air war grips you, the
next dossier opens another file the histories rush past — subscribe and turn on the bell. This
was The War Dossier.

When a wounded German was taken by American forces, he often found himself swept into the exact
same medical chain as a wounded American GI. A medic — sometimes the very same medic who had,
minutes earlier, been treating the American in the next foxhole — would kneel beside him in the
mud, stop his bleeding, dress his wound, and tag him for evacuation. He would be carried back

impossible to process. Interrogators, chaplains, and medical staff recorded the reactions again
and again: first confusion, then suspicion, and then a kind of shaken, disbelieving silence.

wept openly. Many simply could not understand why the men they had been shooting at were now
working, with evident care, to keep them alive.

capability behind it. The American military medical system was a machine of survival operating on
a scale that the German army — especially in the war's later years, as its own supplies
collapsed — could no longer come close to matching. And at the very heart of that machine was a
substance that struck men used to watching wounds turn fatal as something close to magic:
penicillin.

[MID-ROLL]

For almost the whole of human history, the great killer of soldiers had not been the wound itself
but what followed it — infection. A survivable injury would fester, sepsis would set in, and the
soldier would die days later, far from the battlefield, of something the wound had merely
started. German military medicine, increasingly stripped of resources, still lost enormous
numbers of men exactly this way. The Americans, by 1944, were mass-producing penicillin in
industrial quantities and shipping it all the way to the front lines. German prisoners watched,
with disbelief, as wounds they knew from grim experience should have been a death sentence simply
closed and healed. To men who had buried comrades killed by far smaller injuries, it looked like
sorcery.

And it was not only the penicillin. The entire system astonished them. Plasma and whole blood,
stored, transported, and given by transfusion right up near the front, pulled men back from the
kind of shock that had always meant death. Mobile surgical hospitals could perform major
operations within hours of a man being hit, close to the fighting, instead of after a long and
fatal delay. There were dedicated evacuation routes, even evacuation aircraft. There was a
seemingly bottomless abundance of clean bandages, of drugs, of plasma, of trained and competent
hands — an abundance of everything, at the precise moment when the German wounded were
increasingly going without any of it.

For the German prisoner, the field hospital became a window into a truth his own leaders had
worked hard to hide from him. He had been told that Germany was winning, or at the very least
holding firm. He had been told the Americans were soft, materialistic, decadent, unwilling to
endure hardship or sacrifice. And now here he lay, in a clean cot, his infected wound healing
before his eyes, surrounded by a wealth of medical supplies his own army could only dream of,
being cared for, patiently, by the very enemy he had been taught to despise. The contradiction
between the propaganda and the cot beneath him was total, and for many men it was overwhelming.

The American interrogators noticed something useful in all of this. A German soldier who had been
saved by American medicine was very often a changed man. The hatred drained out of a great many
of them. Some became willing to talk freely, not out of fear, but because the experience had
shattered the entire picture of the world they had carried into the war. The kindness had not
been designed as a weapon — it flowed from a simple, stubborn principle, that a wounded man is a
patient first and an enemy second — but it functioned as one of the most effective weapons
imaginable. It broke their belief in the cause they were bleeding for.

and clean bandages. The abundance that won the battles also healed the wounded — on both sides.
It was all the same machine, the same overflowing productive power, simply pointed at a different
task. A nation that could produce so much that it had enough left over to save its own enemies

has more than he can use.

German officers were, by training and by temperament, men of order. They believed in clear
chains of command, in precise orders flowing cleanly from the top downward, in discipline
expressed through control. So when they began to study how the Americans actually used their
radios, many of them were genuinely, deeply baffled. What they heard sounded like pure chaos.
Casual voices. Constant, overlapping chatter. Sergeants talking directly to pilots. Tank crews
talking directly to infantry. Plain language, even slang, where the Germans expected formal

To understand the gap, you have to understand two fundamentally different philosophies of war.
The German army was brilliant — its doctrine of fast, aggressive maneuver had conquered most of
a continent. But its command culture, especially under the strain of total war and an
increasingly meddlesome high command, tended to concentrate decisions upward. Orders were
detailed and comprehensive. Initiative existed, famously so at times, but the overall system
leaned toward control from above. And critically, beneath the doctrine, Germany never had enough
radios. Many German tanks early in the war carried only a receiver, not a transmitter — they
could be told what to do, but could not easily talk back. Whole units depended on signal flags,
on runners carrying messages on foot, and on field telephones whose wires were constantly being

The Americans went the other way almost without consciously deciding to. They were a nation of
mass production and, just as importantly, a nation thoroughly comfortable with machines, with
improvisation, and with ordinary people making decisions. And so they did something that seems
obvious in hindsight but was quietly revolutionary in practice: they put a radio in very nearly
everything, and then — this is the crucial part — they let people actually use them.

[MID-ROLL]

Almost every American tank had a radio that could both send and receive. Infantry platoons
carried portable sets down to a low level. Forward observers had them. And crucially, the
Americans deliberately built the ability for completely different kinds of units to talk directly
to one another. An infantry officer pinned down by a German strongpoint could get on the radio
and speak, directly, to the tanks that could destroy it, or to the artillery that could flatten
it, or even to the aircraft circling overhead. He did not have to send a request crawling up a
long chain of command and then wait, helpless, for permission to come crawling back down.

This is what the German officers were hearing, and fundamentally misreading. That casual,
constant chatter was not a sign of indiscipline. It was a decentralized command system operating
at the speed of human conversation. The American who sounded so relaxed on the radio was, in that
relaxed voice, making a real decision in real time — coordinating tanks, guns, and aircraft on
the spot, on his own authority, without pausing to ask anyone above him. The German officer,
listening to the intercepts, applied his own standards of what proper military communication
should sound like, and concluded the enemy was simply sloppy. What he was actually witnessing was
an army that had pushed authority downward, that trusted the man on the spot to see the situation
and act on it.

The effect on the battlefield was profound, and it showed up most clearly in speed. When a German
unit launched a counterattack, the American response could come from every direction at once,
summoned by a handful of voices on the radio net — the infantry holding firm, the tanks shifting
to meet the thrust, the artillery already firing, the aircraft already rolling in. The entire
American combat machine could pivot to face a new threat in a matter of minutes. The German
attack, dependent on a slower, more fragile, more centralized communications web, repeatedly
found itself reacting to an enemy that seemed, impossibly, to think and move faster than it
possibly could.

There is real and specific testimony to this from the German side. Commanders complained, with
evident frustration, that the Americans recovered from being surprised with an almost unnatural
speed — that you could catch them completely off guard, achieve a clean local surprise, and still
find, within minutes, that the full weight of their firepower had been gathered up and redirected
straight onto you. They attributed it sometimes to luck, sometimes to the Americans' bottomless
material abundance. But the deeper cause was the network of voices, and the trust that allowed
even low-ranking men to use it to make decisions that mattered.

The Germans also badly underestimated something subtler: the value of all that constant, casual
chatter for simple awareness. Because everyone on the net was talking, everyone on the net knew
what was happening. A tank commander could overhear that the infantry on his left were suddenly
under heavy pressure, and move to support them before any formal order ever arrived. The "noise"
that the German officers scorned as undisciplined was, in fact, a shared picture of the entire
battlefield, updating itself second by second in the minds of everyone who was listening. It was
situational awareness, broadcast continuously, for free.

It is worth being fair to the Germans here. They were not fools about communications — far from
it. They had pioneered the bold use of radio in their early tank victories, and they understood
its power as well as anyone. But they were caught in a vise between a command culture that leaned
toward control and a material shortage that denied them radios in the numbers they truly needed.
The Americans, blessed with both abundance and a more decentralized national instinct, simply
out-communicated them — not with better discipline in the German sense of the word, but with more
voices, more trust, and more raw speed.

So why couldn't German officers understand American radio discipline? Because they were measuring
it with entirely the wrong instrument. They were searching for control, and what they saw looked
like chaos. But the chaos was the whole point. The Americans had discovered, perhaps without ever
fully articulating it, that in the fast and confused reality of modern battle, an army that lets a
thousand men talk and decide for themselves will always out-react an army that waits for one man
to issue the command. The casual American voice on the radio was never a sign of weakness. It was
